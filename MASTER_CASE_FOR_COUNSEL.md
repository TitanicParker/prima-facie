# MASTER CASE FOR COUNSEL

**Independent documentary case | Galway neurological admission, 3–11 July 2017 | clinical and institutional sequence to October 2026**

**Status:** Evidence-led counsel briefing and prima facie case analysis, **not** a judicial finding, expert negligence opinion, or allegation that deliberate falsification is proved. Prepared from the supplied clinical canonical transcription, the three manuscript images and their accompanying readable transcription, supplied case-register material, and identified published legal authorities. The source documents remain controlling. Sensitive personal details are omitted from this public-repository-ready narrative; counsel should keep identified clinical exhibits access-controlled.

**Primary question:** *Identify the dated examination, findings, differential reasoning, conclusion, follow-up allocation and communication that answered the GP's referral concerning painful feet, toe-clawing, abnormal gait and increased muscle tension. If none is identified, explain how the resulting incomplete explanatory framework was recorded, carried forward, challenged and institutionally reviewed.*

## Executive case statement

On 29 June 2017 the GP referred a connected and deteriorating neurological presentation. It involved painful feet and toe-clawing together with abnormal gait, rigidity, increased tone and worsening dexterity. The hospital assessed Parkinsonism, documented a lower-limb posture suggestive of dystonia rather than genuine foot drop, and treated leg tension with clonazepam. It also recorded structural foot pathology and arranged Podiatry input. Those were real clinical activities. **The surviving documentation does not, however, demonstrate a completed, reasoned neurological resolution of the relationship between the painful toe-clawing and the movement disorder.**

The treating neurologist's three original handwritten interview pages document a longstanding condition, an earlier 2014 presentation with arm-and-leg tension, the patient's belief that the disabling 2017 condition represented the maturation of the earlier symptoms, and a change in pain after he left bar work in 2012. Reduced standing-related foot pain made persistent leg tension easier to distinguish. **A distinction between sensations is not a diagnosis of separate diseases.** The final impression attributed a marked separation to the patient and offered possible secondary peripheral neuropathy while expressly saying the relationship was **“Not clear.”** The management plan then referred **“As per above.”**

The patient gives firsthand evidence that on 10 July 2017, shortly before discharge, he asked four medical students to summon the neurologist and protested that his feet had not been adequately investigated. The completed account does not record the objection or an identifiable targeted response. That juxtaposition has a documentary footprint: the earlier patient-reported symptom distinction is preserved and given clinical interpretative weight; the later challenge to the scope of investigation is not preserved. This does not itself prove when any paragraph was drafted or why.

The subsequent records show continuing pain, motor treatment, neurological medication-linked foot phenomena, later levodopa-associated pain relief and severe psychological distress. The hospital's 2025–2026 response defended substantial elements of clinical management and communicated a conclusion of no deficit. The disclosed outcome correspondence does not itself establish that reviewers independently tested the founding referral or the transformation and attribution of the clinical history; the underlying PAR/SIMT materials remain outstanding.

**Prima facie finding:** **Yes**, there is a concrete documentary case requiring a substantive answer about referral completion, diagnostic attribution, continuity and governance. **Qualified yes**, there is an apparent clinical-breach question requiring independent movement-disorders evidence under Irish law. **Not yet established:** deliberate falsification, a particular negligent prescription date, that all pain was dystonic, or the amount of injury attributable to any breach. The case does not require those propositions to ask its central question.

### What the manuscript establishes and does not establish

Page 1 records deteriorating coordinated walking, handwriting, balance and dexterity, and a 2014 presentation involving physical arm-and-leg tension. Page 2 reports the patient's assessment that his condition was the **“maturation”** of that earlier presentation; it also describes long-standing bodily tension and self-adopted postures to obtain relaxation. Page 3 describes corns, calluses and fallen arches, treatment since approximately 2009–2010, leaving bar work in 2012 and reduced foot pain; persistent leg tension was then easier to distinguish.

The crucial distinction is between **phenomenology** (different sensations and time courses) and **aetiology** (different underlying diseases). Relief after reduced time standing supports a mechanical contribution to painful pressure lesions; it cannot, unaided, exclude abnormal neurologically driven posture as a contributor. The 2014 history supports the presence of a prior syndrome, not a proven continuous pathophysiological diagnosis. Neurology's later observation and clonazepam treatment make that possible continuity a clinically testable question.

### The single interview represented in different documentary forms

| Level | Document, date and author | Key evidence | Meaning and limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Original account | Three original handwritten history pages from the treating neurologist's 3 July interview | 2014 arm-and-leg tension; **“Liam feels this current state is the maturation of that”**; leaving bar work reduced foot pain; tension distinguishable | Patient-reported continuity and differentiated bodily experience, **not** a patient diagnosis of separate illnesses. |
| Later history | Typed clinical history/discharge material attributed to treating clinician; date of first typing not proven | `CLIN-000965–000999` includes account of different problems/sensations and maturation | A narrative summary; exact transformation must be checked against manuscript rather than assuming phrase invented later. |
| Final clinical inference | Neurologist's impression in July discharge synthesis | **“in his own mind - markedly divorced”** (`CLIN-001146–001149`) | Adds doctor-authored characterization of patient's *mental separation*. May appear to place diagnostic partition on the patient. |
| Alternative diagnosis | Same impression | **“may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy”** and **“Not clear”** (`CLIN-001142–001149`) | Explicitly conditional differential; cannot be recited later as a confirmed neuropathy work-up. |
| Authorised management account | Discharge recommendation and 17 July confirmation | **“As per above”** (`CLIN-001614`); confirmation `CLIN-000261` | The uncertain synthesis became usable as the official prospective clinical description, although uncertainty did not disappear linguistically. |

The clinical risk is **source-derived corroboration**: the same interview, rendered as handwriting, typed history and impression, can appear to offer independent support for separation when it is in fact a single doctor-mediated interpretative chain. It was the clinician's responsibility to distinguish patient testimony, clinical inference and positive findings.

### Epistemically unresolved; operationally settled

The clinician did not write that peripheral neuropathy had been proven. He wrote that it *might* explain the residual foot symptoms and that the relationship was **“Not clear.”** Yet the completed discharge structure made that explanation available to later management. In plain terms: **the doctor did not establish what caused the feet, but the record provided a category in which they could be treated as separate.** This is a testable effect, not proof that every later note repeated the label or that every later action was caused exclusively by it.

### The importance of authorisation and intention

The neurologist authored or authorised the final clinical account and is responsible for the wording selected, the attribution to the patient and the clinical reasoning it conveys. That responsibility can be tested without alleging the author secretly intended to protect himself. A defensive function or intentional concealment is a separate inference dependent on chronology, version logs, correspondence and any evidence about the protest. **Do not infer post-protest first drafting from the 17 July confirmation alone.** Outstanding Article 15 **Object A** seeks the actual creation and revision history and is already requested.

## The interception — 10 July 2017

The patient recounts that after learning discharge was imminent on Monday 10 July, he asked four students to summon the neurologist and protested that the original foot problem had not been adequately investigated. That is direct firsthand evidence with a possible witness trail. The completed account records his earlier differentiation of sensations, intensifies that distinction in the final impression, and contains no corresponding account of the later objection. This is the **documentary footprint of an omitted challenge**: an absence that can be verified, beside positive material that the author retained.

The hospital at this point knew of the patient's reported historical tension and the neurologist's own dystonic-looking movement observation; clonazepam had been used for leg tension. Even without proof that the doctor personally read the full GP letter on a particular morning, the hospital had information capable of reopening the foot-related differential. An appropriate response could have been targeted examination, explanation of uncertainty, movement-disorders input, a specific outpatient review or documented referral ownership. It need not have been immediate levodopa.

The question for clinical experts is whether discharge without a reasoned account or explicit handover plan was below ordinary specialist care in July 2017. The questions for fact witnesses are the conversation and what followed; absence from the discharge account must not be treated as evidence the protest never occurred. Retain the original student/ward-source requests and do not invent witness testimony.

### The long-term clinical test

Treatment of Parkinsonism does not establish that the painful foot syndrome was fully characterised. Procyclidine withdrawal, levodopa-associated foot relief and subsequent Sinemet CR benefit are relevant challenges to the original partition. They do **not** identify with certainty the mechanism of every corn, callus or period of pain. A rigorous expert should distinguish fixed structural lesions, dystonic posturing, off-period pain, peripheral neuropathic symptoms and coexisting mechanisms, as well as dopaminergic treatment risks. **The later record contains actual reconsideration**—notably explicit discussion of painful dystonia and a controlled-release levodopa trial—so it is inaccurate to allege that no clinician ever reconsidered the feet. The narrower unresolved point is that no traceable, reasoned final answer to the original linked referral appears in the surviving record.

## The Prima Facie Case

**Meaning:** *Prima facie* is used here in the practical evidential sense of an apparent, specifically documented case that calls for a substantive answer when the surviving material is read fairly. It is **not** a conclusion that the Irish courts have found breach, causation, damages or intention. In Irish clinical-negligence law, **Dunne v National Maternity Hospital [1989] IR 91** requires assessment by reference to a medical practitioner of comparable general/specialist status exercising ordinary care. Following an accepted practice is normally relevant, although an inherently defective practice is not automatically protected; mere honest disagreement between respectable treatment approaches is insufficient. See *Perez v Coombe Women and Infants University Hospital* [2025] IEHC 396, paragraphs 132–134, reproducing the Dunne principles and *Morrissey v HSE* [2020] IESC 6: https://www.bailii.org/ie/cases/IEHC/2025/2025IEHC396.html . Medical breach and causation ordinarily require independent expert support.

### Legal framing for counsel

A viable professional-negligence claim requires more than a documentary inconsistency: identify duty; conduct below the applicable *Dunne* standard; a feasible alternative investigation or management choice; and injury probably caused by that difference. A response to the original referral may include honest residual uncertainty coupled with appropriate follow-up. If expert evidence considers July 2017 management adequate, the case may narrow to later missed review points or records-governance duties. Conversely, the strongest documentary criticism does not depend on asserting immediate levodopa, a single dystonia cause for all foot pain, or improper subjective intention.

## Final conclusion

**A clinician's documented uncertainty can be a legitimate discharge outcome if the uncertainty is appropriately assessed, communicated and managed.** The evidence in this case raises a concrete different question: whether the original linked painful-foot neurological referral received that assessment and management, or whether the patient's differentiated sensations were given the practical force of a separate diagnostic compartment without adequate demonstrated foundation. The surviving record supports the narrower finding that a completed answer cannot be identified; it records meaningful care and later reconsideration without clearly showing the missing closure. A first-person protest, not preserved in the completed account, marks a specific opportunity to confront the omission. The subsequent eight years add evidence and harm; the institutional no-deficit conclusion must be tested against the actual governance review sources. This is a **prima facie documentary and professional-accountability case**, with legally actionable clinical breach and causal injury reserved for competent expert evidence and Irish counsel's assessment.
